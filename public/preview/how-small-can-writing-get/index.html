Do some writing systems say more with less?

Every writing system strikes a bargain. English uses a small set of simple symbols and needs many of them to say anything. Chinese uses thousands of complex symbols and needs far fewer. This page asks whether either one carries more information for the same amount of visual material, whether that material is the pixels a text fills on a screen or the instructions needed to draw it.

This is a plain-language account of the glyphbits project so far, and it assumes no background. Andrew Brook conceived and directed the project; Claude (Anthropic) ran the experiments and wrote the code and the text. This account dates from November 2026, and its conclusions are provisional.

The answer so far

We price visual material in two ways. The first is area: draw the text as pixels, as small as a reader can still manage, and count every pixel it fills. The second is drawing instructions: describe each character as the strokes and curves a pen must make to draw it legibly, and count the bits in that description. Either way, a ratio of 1.0 means that Chinese and English need the same amount for the same texts. A ratio below 1 means that Chinese needs less, and a ratio above 1 means that it needs more. Before measuring, we agreed that “about equal” would mean a ratio between 0.80 and 1.25.

1.16Measured by drawing instructions, Chinese needs about 16% more bits than English for the same texts. The figure stays between 1.05 and 1.22 under most settings, inside the band we called about equal.
0.35–2.1Measured by area, the answer depends on what the reader must be able to do and what it knows about the letterforms. Our headline reader gives 0.59, while two people judging fonts drawn pixel by pixel put it near 1.5.

On this evidence the two scripts are about equally efficient when the cost is the instructions needed to draw them, and the question is undecided when the cost is area. Both measures rest on a judgment of what counts as legible, and area depends on it far more. We are now building a reader calibrated to published experiments on people, and we will state a conclusion only after both measures have been through it (see what comes next).

One caveat could outweigh everything else. Our texts are English originals translated into Chinese, and translation changes length. Where native writers described the same pictures independently, Chinese was 0.98 the length of English rather than 0.70. Both measures grow with the length of the text, so fairer texts could raise both ratios by as much as 40%.

Many simple symbols, or a few complex ones

English and Chinese use very different numbers of symbols to say the same thing. In our texts, 960 passages that exist in both languages, Chinese uses about a third as many symbols as English, counting letters, characters, digits, punctuation and spaces.

The price of that economy is a far larger set of symbols. One simple measure of a set’s size is the number of bits needed to give each symbol its own number. A bit is a single yes-or-no choice, so 6 bits can number 64 symbols and 7 bits can number 128; each extra bit doubles the count.

Symbols are not used equally often, so a fairer count weights each one by how often it appears. Named one at a time, a symbol in our English texts carries about 4.5 bits and a Chinese character about 9.2. Chinese thus uses a third as many symbols, each carrying about twice as much information.

Two ways to count the cost

A symbol that packs in more meaning needs a more complex shape, with more strokes and more detail. Counts of symbols and bits say nothing about this cost. The question is whether the extra detail costs about as much as the fewer symbols save, and there are two natural ways to put a price on detail.

Area. Draw the text as a bitmap, shrink it until a reader can only just cope, and count every pixel, blank space included. A complex character needs more pixels before its strokes run together, so area charges for detail through size.

Drawing instructions. Describe each character as a list of pen strokes, made of lines, arcs and curves, with just enough precision for a reader to recognize the result, and count the bits in that list. Area plays no part; a complex character costs more because it has more strokes to describe.

Both measures need a judgment of what counts as legible, and that judgment matters more than anything else on this page. The first part covers area and the second covers drawing instructions.

Part 1

Area: counting pixels

Measured by area, there is no single answer, because it depends on what a reader must be able to do. The three bands below come from the simulated readers described in this part.

0.4–0.9The reader already knows this typeface well and only needs to tell characters apart.
0.9–1.2The reader has learned to compare shapes but has never studied either script.
1.3–2.1There is no reader at all, and every stroke must survive the shrinking.

Before measuring, we agreed on what would count as a solid result: three different simulated readers had to agree to within 25%. They did not (they gave 0.59, 0.57 and 1.08), so we do not declare a winner. Against the band of 0.80–1.25 set in advance for “about equal”, the idea that all writing systems are about equally efficient is neither confirmed nor ruled out when the cost is area.

That uncertainty is itself the main finding of this part: a writing system has no single “smallest bitmap”. There are several reasonable definitions, ranging from “the dots merely differ” to “the text can be read at full speed”. The project now measures a ladder of them and asks how well they agree (see below).

The words we need

Size is measured in pixels per em. The em is the height of the square in which a font is designed. A Chinese character fills its em square almost completely, but a lowercase English letter is only about half an em tall, so at 12 pixels per em a small English letter is only about 6 pixels tall. Pixels per em is a setting, like a zoom level. It says how large the text is drawn, not how much space the text takes.

From text to a number

Every ratio on this page compares areas. We set the same sentence in both scripts and count the pixels in the rectangle each version fills, including the white space between characters and between lines, since a page cannot use that space for anything else. The ratio is the Chinese area divided by the English area, so a ratio below 1 means that Chinese fits the same meaning into fewer pixels.

Fonts lay text out on a grid of ems. Each character moves the pen forward by a fixed fraction of an em, and lines sit a fixed number of ems apart. A sentence is therefore the same number of ems long and the same number of ems tall at every size, and pixels per em converts both measurements into pixels. The width in pixels is the length in ems times the pixels per em. The height of each line is its height in ems times the pixels per em, plus one blank pixel to separate it from the next. Because the size enters both the width and the height, the area grows with its square.

Three ingredients therefore decide every ratio. Line length and line height belong to the scripts and fonts, and they are the same whichever reader is used. Pixels per em is the one we measure: we shrink the text until a reader fails, and the size at which it fails is that reader’s limit.

For example, our headline reader, the ideal observer described below, reaches its limit at 10.85 pixels per em for English and 9.53 for Chinese. Take an English passage that is 100 ems long; the same passage in Chinese is 70 ems long. At its limit the English line is 100 × 10.85 = 1,085 pixels wide and 1.01 × 10.85 + 1 ≈ 12.0 pixels tall, so it covers about 13,000 pixels. The Chinese line is 70 × 9.53 ≈ 667 pixels wide and 1.10 × 9.53 + 1 ≈ 11.5 pixels tall, so it covers about 7,700 pixels. The ratio is 7,700 ÷ 13,000 ≈ 0.59. The length of the passage cancels out, so the whole calculation fits on one line: 0.70 × 9.53 × (1.10 × 9.53 + 1) ÷ (10.85 × (1.01 × 10.85 + 1)) ≈ 0.59.

These limits describe outline fonts shrunk by a computer, not writing as such. Designers drawing pixel by pixel do far better in English: Andrew finds Tom Thumb, a font with 6 pixels per em, easy to read, at little more than half the ideal observer’s limit. They gain much less in Chinese, where the smallest complete font uses 8 pixels per em and separates many characters by a single pixel. The section on fonts drawn by hand returns to this.

From machine learning: build readers

We built three simulated readers.

  • The ideal observer. It stores a picture of every character, compares the bitmap with each one, and answers with the closest match. It knows the typeface at other weights, and it is our headline rule.
  • The literate network. This small neural network is trained separately at every size, like a student of a single script.
  • The novice network. This network was trained on Hangul, Hebrew, Thai and 13 other scripts to judge whether two pictures show the same character. It then meets Latin and Chinese for the first time. Neither script was in its lessons, so neither should enjoy a home advantage, although it may still have learned shapes closer to one of them.

Measuring what is lost

We do not simply count right and wrong answers. Losing one Chinese character in a hundred loses far more than losing one English letter, because a Chinese character carries about twice as much information. Think of the game of twenty questions: it takes about 4.7 yes-or-no questions to pin down an English character in our texts and 9.3 to pin down a Chinese one, not counting spaces. Each question is one bit.

We therefore ask what share of those bits has been lost. We set the limit at 2% lost and report other limits alongside it, because the result depends on this choice too.

What we observed

1. The readers disagree

A pattern emerges. The less a reader already knows the letterforms, the more Chinese tends to cost. We noticed this only afterwards, so it is a hypothesis to test later rather than a result. One run goes against it: the literate network trained on sans and tested on serif gives 0.59, because English suffered more.

What we assumed

What we got wrong

  • Our first forecast. Before measuring, we predicted that Chinese would take 1.1–1.2 times the pixels of English. That forecast used the height of capital letters, although most English text is lowercase. Done properly, the arithmetic gives about 0.55.
  • Other forecasts. We expected the literate network to beat people at the smallest sizes, but it does so only from 8 pixels up. We expected the novice network to come close to people, but it falls well below them. Our forecast for the readers, 0.8–1.4, was mostly wrong: they range from 0.57 to 1.08.
  • A bad measure. Our first measure of lost information favored Chinese heavily. We caught the error when a reader got 38% of characters right while the measure claimed that only 9% of the information was lost. We replaced the measure and added a test.
  • The gates. Before starting, we set three checks and promised to stop if any failed. Two failed: the comparison with Yang’s experiment, and the requirement that the answer move by less than 15% when fonts and noise change (it moved from 0.39 to 0.70). The third passed for gray pictures only. We stopped, and we changed nothing to make the checks pass.

Several kinds of “legible”

The size at which a text stops being usable depends on what the reader must do with it, and each definition asks something different. The table runs from the loosest definition to the strictest.

Comfortable reading. Reading speed rises with print size up to a critical print size and then levels off. A 2019 study measured this size for Chinese and English readers in the same way and found that the comfortable size was 3.4 times the smallest recognizable size in both scripts (Wang, Lin & Guo 2019). If that holds, comfort scales both scripts alike and leaves the ratio where the readable rung puts it.

Fonts drawn by hand

The pictures above were made by shrinking smooth outline fonts and letting a computer decide which squares turn black. Small English comes out messy that way, because g and q, and l and I, run together. Early computers did it differently: a designer drew every character pixel by pixel, judging by eye what stayed readable. Those fonts are a record of human judgment that we can use without testing anyone.

We found openly licensed fonts of this kind for both scripts. The smallest English fonts with both capitals and lowercase use a 4 × 6 cell. The smallest Chinese font that covers all 6,763 characters of the standard simplified set uses 8 × 8. Genuine Chinese dot-matrix fonts from the 1980s used 16 × 16.

Andrew drew his own compact fonts in the 1990s. They were lost with a floppy disk, but he remembered their limits: capitals and digits fit a 3 × 5 grid, and everything else, including lowercase and punctuation, fits 7 × 9. We redrew them for this project with the simplest shapes that stay clean, and checked by machine that no two characters are identical.

What people manage

We ran no tests on people, but published experiments cover part of the same ground. In one, native readers named Chinese characters cut to black-and-white grids by computer, exactly as our readers see them: they named 70% correctly at 8 × 8, 92% at 10 × 10 and 98% at 12 × 12 (Yang 2015). Our ideal observer’s Chinese limit of 9.53 pixels per em falls between the last two. For English the observer is cautious. People named capitals in 3 × 5 dot fonts drawn by designers almost as accurately as in 5 × 7 (Shurtleff 1970), and read grayscale English nearly perfectly at about 7–9 pixels per em, below the observer’s 10.85.

No experiment has compared the two scripts in black-and-white fonts as small as Tom Thumb, so the judgments of two readers stand alone. Andrew, a native English speaker who once read Chinese moderately well, finds Tom Thumb much easier than the 8 × 8 Chinese font and about as easy as 10 × 10 or 12 × 12 Chinese. A second reader, a native English speaker who reads Chinese fairly well, put the match near 10 × 10. With a third as many characters per text, that equivalence implies an area ratio of about 1.4–1.6.

The likeliest reason is that a black-and-white grid charges for every stroke. A 3 × 5 letter keeps all its strokes, since even E needs only five rows, while an 8 × 8 Chinese character must merge or drop some of them, and readers who know the characters at full detail have never learned the abbreviations. This mechanism, if it holds, matters for both measures, and it is what the calibrated reader will test.

Part 2

Drawing instructions: counting bits

The second measure ignores area. We traced the center line of every character in the same two typefaces, broke it into straight lines, circular arcs and curves to within a fixed tolerance, and placed their ends on a grid. Each piece is written down as its type and a few numbers, and we count the bits in that list. The grid is as fine as the readers of part 1 need at their limit: 10 steps across an English em and 12 across a Chinese one.

Counted as strokes, Chinese costs 40–60% more than English. Counted as bits, the gap narrows to 16%, because Chinese strokes are simpler: 87% of them are straight, against 62% in English. The figure in bits is also far steadier than any area figure. Across five tolerances and eleven grids it runs from 1.07 to 1.19, and pen data drawn by hand, rather than traced from fonts, give 1.15.

Three limits apply. The description treats every character on its own, so a component that appears in hundreds of Chinese characters is paid for each time, and a code that reused parts would favor Chinese. The tracing is faithful to the font, which is not the same as the simplest drawing a reader would accept. And the grid comes from the area readers of part 1, so the drawing measure inherits some of their uncertainty, although little, because both scripts need nearly the same grid.

What comes next

  • A reader calibrated to people. We are fitting a simulated reader to two published experiments and testing it, unchanged, against seven others. It will know the characters but not the font, as people do. It will then judge the fonts drawn by hand and stand in for people in scripts that have no comparable experiments. We have written down its prediction in advance: by area, Chinese will need 1.3–2.2 times the pixels of English in fonts drawn by hand.
  • Instructions to draw legibly. Instead of tracing each character faithfully, we will simplify the drawing until the calibrated reader can no longer name it, and count the bits at that point. We will compare stroke endpoints, simple shapes with their parameters and curves with their control points, and we will let the description reuse parts that many characters share.
  • The rest of the ladder. We will measure the readable, designer’s-limit and comfortable kinds of legibility, writing down a prediction for each first. Seventeen openly licensed bitmap fonts are ready, along with two recreated ones.
  • Fair texts. We will build a new collection with originals in every language, so that English no longer sets the length.
  • More scripts. Cyrillic, Arabic and Devanagari come next, measured both ways.

Words, in one place

Bit
A bit is one yes-or-no question’s worth of information.
Bitmap
A bitmap is a picture made of a grid of pixels.
Bitmap font
A bitmap font is drawn pixel by pixel at one size, as on early computers, rather than shrunk from an outline.
Bootstrap interval
A bootstrap interval comes from redrawing the texts at random many times to see how much the result depends on which texts we happened to pick. Our intervals are about ±2%, so they say nothing about the much larger differences between rules.
Drawing instructions
Drawing instructions are a list of the strokes and curves a pen must make to draw a character, written as numbers whose bits can be counted.
Critical print size
The critical print size is the smallest print at which reading speed stops improving with size.
Designer’s limit
The designer’s limit is the smallest size at which someone has published a complete, well-regarded bitmap font.
Em, pixels per em
The em is the square in which a font is drawn, and pixels per em is the number of pixels along its side.
Gate
A gate is a check, agreed in advance, that must pass before the work goes on.
Ideal observer
An ideal observer is a reader that always gives the best possible answer from what it can see.
Information lost
Information lost is the share of the bits in a character that the bitmap no longer conveys. We measure it as an upper bound.
Legible, readable
Text is legible when each character can be told from the others on its own, and readable when the text as a whole can be read with the help of context.
Network, seed
A network is a trained pattern recognizer, and its seed is the random starting point of its training.
Serif, sans
Serif typefaces have small feet on their strokes; sans typefaces do not.
Threshold
The threshold is how much ink a square must hold before it turns black.
Topology
Topology here means the number of separate pieces and holes in a shape.
Weight
A typeface’s weight is the thickness of its strokes.
